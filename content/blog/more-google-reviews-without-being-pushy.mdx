The single biggest factor in getting more reviews is not what you say, it is when you ask. A message sent 24 hours after a completed job, with a direct link and no pressure, converts far better than any in-person request.

## Why timing beats wording

Right after a job, the customer is still deciding how they feel about the experience. A day later, once everything works and the relief has set in, they are in the best possible mood to leave a positive review. Ask too early and the job is not finished emotionally. Ask too late and they have moved on entirely.

## The message that works

Keep it short, personal and specific to the job. Something like: "Thanks again for choosing us for your boiler service today. If you have a minute, a Google review really helps other people in the area find us. Here is a direct link." A generic, obviously templated message performs worse than one that references the actual job.

## Automate it, do not rely on memory

Even with good intentions, manually remembering to message every customer at the right time eventually breaks down when things get busy. Setting up an automatic text or email that fires 24 hours after a job is marked complete removes that risk entirely and means every customer gets asked, not just the ones you happened to remember.

## What to do about a negative review

Respond calmly, acknowledge the specific issue, and offer to resolve it outside the public thread if appropriate. A thoughtful response to a negative review often builds more trust with future customers than having no negative reviews at all, because it shows how the business handles a real problem.

## How many reviews is enough

There is no fixed number, but businesses with 50 or more recent, relevant reviews consistently outperform those with under 20 in the same local market. The goal is steady growth over time, not a single push to hit a number and then stop.
